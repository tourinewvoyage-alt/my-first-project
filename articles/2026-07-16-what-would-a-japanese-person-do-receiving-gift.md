*Okaeshi (お返し) — "the return." In Japan, a gift is never just a gift; it's the opening move in a graceful, lifelong game of balance. Here are the rules nobody will ever explain to you out loud.*

## The Scene

<!-- 🖼 画像1(無料部分): デパート包装のきれいな箱(お菓子を1つ買って自分で撮るのが簡単)。Unsplash検索例: "japanese gift wrapping" -->

You visit your Japanese friend Yuki's family home for dinner, and you bring a small gift from your country — a box of chocolates, maybe some local coffee. Nothing serious. Yuki's mother receives it with both hands, bows, thanks you warmly… and sets it aside, unopened.

First surprise: *she didn't open it.* In your country, tearing the wrapping open on the spot and exclaiming over the contents is half the fun. Here, the box sits quietly on a side table all evening, like a guest too polite to speak.

Second surprise arrives two weeks later. A package: beautifully wrapped rice crackers from a famous shop, addressed to you, from Yuki's mother. The value is — and this will turn out to matter — roughly half of what your chocolates cost.

Third surprise: when you thank Yuki, she winces slightly and says, "Ah, you didn't have to mention it." 

You have just brushed against one of the most intricate systems in Japanese social life, and nobody will ever hand you the manual.

## The Question

Why not open the gift? Why the return package? Why *half* the value? And why was thanking her the slightly wrong move?

None of this is coldness. Every one of these moves is calibrated, considerate, and ancient. The question is: what's the logic?

## The Short Answer

The logic is balance. A gift creates a small, warm debt; *okaeshi* — the return gift — settles it at a precise exchange rate, so the relationship stays level and can continue forever.

Below the line: the unwritten rulebook — when gifts are opened and why, the half-value rule (*hangaeshi*), the two great gift seasons, what counts as a dangerous gift, and how to play this game as a foreigner without stepping on any of its invisible tripwires.

## What Actually Happens: The Unwritten Rulebook

### Rule 1: The gift is received with ceremony, opened in private

Yuki's mother took your chocolates with both hands and a bow — the object was honored. But opening it in front of you risks several small disasters: her face might betray disappointment; comparing gifts becomes possible if others brought something; and reacting on the spot puts performance pressure on both sides. Opening later, alone, protects everyone. (With younger people and close friends, this is relaxing — many will now ask, "May I open it?" — but the default with elders remains: later.)

### Rule 2: The debt must be returned — at half value

*Okaeshi* is near-mandatory, and its exchange rate is startlingly specific: *hangaeshi* (半返し), "half return." Wedding gifts of cash are answered with catalog gifts worth about half. Your chocolates were answered with crackers at roughly 50%.

Why half, not equal? Equal return would *close* the relationship — debt fully settled, ledger at zero, nothing owed, nothing continuing. It can even read as: "we're done." Half-return honors the debt while leaving warmth in the account. And returning *more* than you received is worse — it reverses the debt and starts an arms race of escalating politeness.

### Rule 3: There are seasons for gratitude

Twice a year, Japan performs society-wide gift maintenance: *ochūgen* (お中元, midsummer) and *oseibo* (お歳暮, year-end) — gifts sent to those you're indebted to: bosses, mentors, doctors, in-laws. Department stores transform into gift-logistics operations. The gifts themselves are deliberately practical — premium cooking oil, beer, fruit, seaweed. The content whispers; the act speaks.

### Rule 4: Some gifts are coded — and some are dangerous

The system has tripwires worth knowing:

- **Numbers:** sets of four (*shi* sounds like death) and nine (*ku* sounds like suffering) are avoided.
- **White flowers and lilies** lean funerary.
- **A handkerchief** can imply parting or tears; **a comb** (*kushi*) contains both unlucky sounds.
- **Shoes or socks to a superior** can suggest "trample on this" — treading on the person.
- **Green tea** used to be a funeral-return staple, so some older people avoid gifting it casually.

No one will be offended if a foreigner errs here — but they'll be quietly impressed if you don't.

### Rule 5: Gratitude is expressed on the next meeting, lightly

Why did Yuki wince? Because directly discussing a gift's arrival edges toward accounting — and the whole system works by *pretending not to be accounting*. The graceful move is a light touch at the next encounter: *"Senjitsu wa gochisōsama deshita"* — thank you for the other day. Acknowledged, not itemized.

## The "Why" Behind It

**1. Giri — the web of mutual obligation.** *Giri* (義理) is often translated "duty," but it's better understood as the maintained web of reciprocal bonds that holds Japanese social life together. Gifts are how the web is tightened, repaired, and made visible. An unanswered gift is a loose thread; okaeshi ties it off — at half tension, so the web stays springy.

**2. The relationship matters more than the object.** Notice that every rule points away from the *stuff* and toward the *bond*. Don't open it now (the object shouldn't upstage the meeting). Return half (keep the bond alive). Give cooking oil (the act is the message). Western gift culture often celebrates the perfect, personal, surprising object; Japanese gift culture celebrates the perfectly *maintained relationship*, with objects as its currency.

**3. Form is kindness.** To outsiders, this can look exhausting — so many rules! But rules are exactly what make the system kind. Because the exchange rate is known (half), nobody lies awake wondering what's appropriate. Because the seasons are fixed, nobody wonders *when*. The form absorbs the anxiety so the people don't have to. This is a deep pattern in Japan, and we'll meet it again and again: **etiquette is not the opposite of warmth; it's warmth, standardized.**

## Useful Phrases

| 日本語 | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| つまらないものですが | Tsumaranai mono desu ga | "It's a trifling thing, but…" (classic humble gift phrase) |
| 心ばかりですが | Kokoro bakari desu ga | "Just a small token of my feelings…" (warmer, modern) |
| 開けてもいいですか? | Akete mo ii desu ka? | May I open it? |
| お口に合うといいのですが | O-kuchi ni au to ii no desu ga | I hope it suits your taste |
| 先日はありがとうございました | Senjitsu wa arigatō gozaimashita | Thank you for the other day |

## The Takeaway

If you give a gift in Japan: hand it over with both hands, downplay it, and don't expect it to be opened. If you *receive* one: a return of roughly half value, a few weeks later, keeps you in perfect standing — and a light "thank you for the other day" at the next meeting completes the dance.

The idea worth keeping: **reciprocity with a small remainder.** Never fully settle the account with people you care about. The unpaid half is where the friendship lives.
